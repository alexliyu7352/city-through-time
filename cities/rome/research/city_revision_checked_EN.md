## P — Prologue

An emperor’s lake disappears, and the Colosseum rises on its ground. How did Rome come from a settlement beside the Tiber, and live through everything that followed?

## 01 — The Hill and the Name

In the legend, Rome begins with 2 brothers who cannot agree about their new city. It is 753 BCE. Romulus and Remus quarrel; Remus is killed, and Romulus is left to rule. The city bears the survivor’s name. Beside the Tiber, people are making homes on the Palatine. A hill, some houses, a name. Rome has so little ground to stand on, and already a death in the story of its birth.

## 02 — The Prize of Victory

Under the Republic, armies leave this city and carry Roman power across Italy, then farther around the Mediterranean. Conquest and diplomacy bring more lands under its rule. Wealth travels back to Rome, and the city that sent its armies out now receives the rewards. But the wealth divides its people: some grow rich while others become poorer, and political rivals turn to civil war.

The struggle reaches a meeting of the Senate in Pompey’s Curia. In 44 BCE, Julius Caesar, who has made himself dictator, is killed there. His death leaves the fight for Rome unfinished. The men who follow him keep fighting until Octavian wins control. Rome has gained dominion far beyond its walls, while the Republic at its centre is slipping away.

## 03 — The Emperor’s Ground

Octavian receives the title Augustus from the Senate. The capital now lives under a single ruler, whose likeness appears in stone and whose city has new baths and public buildings. Water and food supplies improve. Writers celebrate Rome’s past, and public religious ceremonies return. After the civil wars, there is a city here worth enjoying: water to use, places to gather, books in which to find a past.

In 64, fire starts among the shops beside the Circus Maximus. It spreads through the lower districts and climbs the hills. The capital’s streets become paths for the flames. At the foot of the Esquiline, buildings are pulled down ahead of them, opening a gap in the city. For a time, the fire’s advance stops there.

Nero’s Golden House follows the fire, with an artificial lake laid out in this valley. Then the water gives way to the Colosseum. Walls rise where the lake lay, and when Titus opens the arena in 80, the public gathers on ground once kept as an emperor’s garden. Beyond it, Trajan’s conquests help pay for his forum and column. Rome seems to have so much still ahead of it.

## 04 — A City Without the Court

The city that reached so far now has to draw a new line around itself. In 271, Aurelian begins its walls. Rome remains spread within them, its palaces and streets still in place. Across the Tiber, Constantine’s age brings old Saint Peter’s Basilica into being. When Alaric turns towards Rome, the western court is in Ravenna, far from these walls and the palaces within them.

## 05 — The Conquered City

Alaric’s army stands outside Rome, and negotiations fail. In 410, his troops break through the city’s defences and plunder it. For centuries, Roman armies have carried this danger towards other people’s homes. Now it has reached their own. Jerome hears what has happened and puts the shock into words: the city which had taken the whole world has itself been taken.

During another siege, in 537, the aqueducts are cut. On the Janiculum, the mills lose the water that drives them. The houses are still standing, and the Tiber still runs below the hill. Above it, the mill wheels stop turning.

## 06 — The Journeys Towards Rome

Across the medieval centuries, the inhabited city gathers towards the Tiber. Its great walls enclose much more ground than its people now occupy. Older buildings and ruins stand beyond their homes, still enclosed by the ancient walls. Yet old Saint Peter’s still brings people across the river. Charlemagne comes for his coronation in 800; the Jubilee of 1300 draws pilgrims. After the years in Avignon, the papacy returns in 1377. Rome’s armies once went out towards distant lands. Now travellers from far away are coming here.

## 07 — The Work of Generations

By 1506, the basilica that has received travellers for centuries is to be replaced. Julius II begins a new Saint Peter’s on the old site. Nearby, Michelangelo paints the Sistine ceiling, while Raphael and his pupils decorate the papal rooms. Outside, parts of the old church come down as the new building takes shape. Future pilgrims will arrive on this same ground and enter a different basilica.

Before the basilica is finished, the city is sacked again, in 1527. Fire damages the Massimo family’s houses. In the midst of a city making new buildings, this patch of Rome is torn open. In the 1530s, Baldassarre Peruzzi builds new walls on that burned ground.

On the Capitoline, Paul III commissions Michelangelo to remake the square. A stairway brings people up from the streets into a space framed by matching palace fronts, set at an angle. The hill turns its approach towards the modern city, away from the ancient Forum.

Across the city, Sixtus V opens the Strada Felice. The long, straight street connects churches that pilgrims come to visit. Their destinations now lie along a road they can follow through Rome. Seen from above, the street makes a line through the roofs; on foot, it becomes a journey from one church to the next.

At Saint Peter’s, Fontana moves the obelisk into the square in 1586. Above it, the dome is still to be completed. Michelangelo designed it and built as far as the drum, its circular base, but he died before it could rise. Giacomo della Porta and Domenico Fontana carry the work upward. In 1593, the ball and cross stand at the top.

By 1667, Bernini’s colonnades curve around Saint Peter’s Square. Those coming to the basilica now arrive between great sweeps of columns. Work on the Capitoline continues in other hands. Above the river, the dome stands over a city remade in streets, stairs and open squares.

## 08 — The City Becomes a Capital

In 1849, Mameli, a poet fighting with Garibaldi’s defenders of the Roman Republic, is wounded. The city is being fought over again. Italian troops enter through Porta Pia in 1870, ending papal temporal rule. Rome becomes Italy’s capital the following year. The flood of 1870 brings another demand: high walls will reshape the riverbanks. Into this city of palaces, churches and changing shores, the national government moves.

## 09 — What the City Loses

Mussolini clears a view of ancient Rome by demolishing a neighbourhood. The Alessandrino streets give way to the Via dell’Impero, opened in 1932. The monuments stand out beside the new avenue. Between those ancient remains, people had made their homes.

War brings a different destruction. Allied bombs hit San Lorenzo in 1943. Elsewhere, Settimia Spizzichino is arrested and taken from Rome to Auschwitz. When Rome is liberated on 4 June 1944, she is still away. Her return comes in 1945, to Via della Reginella, where she begins to tell what she endured.

## 10 — Making Worlds in Rome

In Roman Holiday, Audrey Hepburn and Gregory Peck make their way through the city’s streets. Foreign producers come to Cinecittà for its studios and the skill of local technicians. Ben Hur’s success in 1959 brings more epic productions. The people working here make worlds for audiences far away, then carry the life of the sets out into the city. Film crews and stars help change Rome’s nightlife.

## 11 — The World Meets Here

On the Capitoline, 6 countries sign the Treaties of Rome in 1957. The capital is receiving the world in peacetime. For the 1960 Olympics, its village receives athletes, with homes for permanent residents to follow after the Games. Later, the Olimpico will host the World Cup final in 1990. Back in 1960, the marathon begins on the Capitoline. Abebe Bikila sets out barefoot. His course passes the Baths of Caracalla and follows the Appian Way, bringing the ancient city into a race watched by the modern world. At the Arch of Constantine, he comes in first, barefoot.

## 12 — The Rome Around the Monuments

The monuments stay where they are, but the city around them keeps reaching farther. Housing spreads beyond the ancient centre into metropolitan Rome. The Colosseum, on the ground of Nero’s lake, is now a point within a much larger city. People are making homes far from the places visitors have come to see.

That spread continues even after the rapid rise in the city’s population has stalled in the late 1960s. Households grow smaller, and people seek more living space for each person. Housing demand carries life farther out through the metropolitan area. After the crisis of 2008, the outward movement slows.

## 13 — Rome Waits

In 2020, the pandemic brings far fewer guests to Rome’s hotels and other lodgings. The rooms and streets are still here. A city so long filled by other people’s journeys is suddenly waiting. On 27 March, Pope Francis prays in the rain before an empty Saint Peter’s Square.

## 14 — Along the River, Beyond the City We Know

For the next great gathering, Rome changes the approach from the river. At Piazza Pia, a new pedestrian connection opens the way from Castel Sant’Angelo towards Saint Peter’s. Through the Jubilee, pilgrims come from 185 countries, received by volunteers and supported by the city’s services. The square fills for the announcement of Leo XIV’s election in 2025. People cross the ground between the river and the basilica again. The Holy Door closes in January 2026, bringing the Jubilee to an end.

Beyond it lies the Tiber, with another possible journey for Rome. A green and blue corridor would follow the river for 75 kilometres, from Settebagni to Ostia Antica. Travellers could move between the historic centre, modern districts, natural places and the countryside along the same water. The hill where we began is still beside the river. Around it stands the whole city. I wonder what we will find here when we come back.
